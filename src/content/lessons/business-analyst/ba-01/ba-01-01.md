> **Prerequisites:** none. This is the first lesson of the Business Analyst path.

## Explanation

### Start with a problem, not a solution

Imagine a hospital where patients wait an average of three hours in A&E. The manager says, *"We need a new software system!"* Maybe they do. Or maybe the real problem is that triage nurses are on the wrong shift pattern, or that test results take too long to reach doctors. Buying software would cost a fortune and fix nothing.

**Business analysis is the discipline of understanding what an organisation really needs, and recommending changes that deliver value.**

The official definition from the IIBA (International Institute of Business Analysis), in its *BABOK Guide*, is:

> *"Business analysis is the practice of enabling change in an enterprise by defining needs and recommending solutions that deliver value to stakeholders."*

Let's unpack the four key words:

| Word | Meaning in plain English |
|---|---|
| **Change** | Something will be different afterwards: a new process, system, product or rule. |
| **Needs** | The real problem or opportunity, not the first solution someone suggests. |
| **Solutions** | Ways to meet the need. Software is only one option; training, a new policy or a simpler process are others. |
| **Stakeholders** | Everyone affected by or interested in the change: customers, staff, managers, IT, regulators. |

### What a business analyst actually does

A business analyst (BA) is a **translator and problem-solver** who stands between "the business" (the people who have the problem) and "the delivery team" (the people who will build or implement the solution).

```
  Business people              Business Analyst              Delivery team
  "We lose customers   --->   understands, questions,  --->  "Build a 2-step
   at checkout"               documents, validates           checkout with
                                                              guest option"
```

Day to day a BA:

1. **Asks questions** to discover the real need ("Why?", "What happens today?", "What would success look like?").
2. **Studies the current process** (the *as-is*).
3. **Defines requirements**: what the solution must do and how well.
4. **Models** processes and data with diagrams so everyone shares the same picture.
5. **Checks** that the delivered solution actually solves the problem.

### Business analyst vs related roles

| Role | Main question they answer |
|---|---|
| **Business Analyst** | *What* does the business need, and *why*? |
| **Project Manager** | *When* will it be delivered, at what *cost*, by *whom*? |
| **Product Owner** (Agile) | *Which* features come first to maximise value? |
| **Data Analyst** | What does the *data* tell us? |
| **Developer / Engineer** | *How* do we build it? |

In small companies one person often wears several of these hats, but knowing the difference helps you explain your role clearly in interviews.

## Real-world examples

| Organisation | Stated request | What the BA discovered | Recommended solution |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Online retailer** | "We need a new website." | 68% of users abandon the cart at the payment step because they are forced to create an account. | Add a guest checkout to the existing site. Cheap and fast. |
| **Bank** | "Automate loan approvals." | Most delays come from missing documents, not slow approval decisions. | A document checklist and upload portal before an application is accepted. |
| **Restaurant chain** | "Staff need a better POS system." | Staff are slow because the menu on the POS doesn't match the printed menu order. | Re-order the POS menu screens and train staff; no new system needed yet. |
| **Council** | "Build an app for bin complaints." | 40% of complaints are about collection days that changed without notice. | SMS alerts for schedule changes plus a simple web form. |

Notice how often the right answer is **smaller and cheaper** than the original request. That is the value of good analysis.

## Step-by-step demonstration

**Scenario:** A small gym says, *"We need a mobile app because members keep cancelling."*

**Step 1: Clarify the problem.** Ask: *How many cancel? Since when? Is it worse than before? What reasons do they give?*
Answer: 15% of members cancelled in the last quarter, up from 8%. Exit forms mention "classes always full" and "can't book".

**Step 2: Understand the as-is process.**
Members book classes by phoning reception. Reception is only staffed 9am-5pm. Popular evening classes fill up during the day while working members can't call.

**Step 3: Identify stakeholders.** Members, reception staff, instructors, the gym owner, possibly an app vendor.

**Step 4: Define the need (not the solution).**
*"Members need to be able to book classes at any time of day, fairly, without phoning."*

**Step 5: Consider options.**

| Option | Cost | Time | Meets the need? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Custom mobile app | High | 4-6 months | Yes |
| Off-the-shelf online booking tool | Low | 2 weeks | Yes |
| Extend reception hours | Medium (staff) | Immediate | Partly |

**Step 6: Recommend.** Start with an off-the-shelf online booking tool, measure cancellations for three months, and revisit a custom app only if needed.

**Step 7: Define success.** *"Cancellation rate back below 10% within one quarter; at least 70% of bookings made online."*

That's business analysis: problem first, evidence second, solution third, measurable success at the end.

## Practical example

Read this request and practise asking BA questions before jumping to a solution:

> *"Our customer support team needs a chatbot."*

Good questions to ask:
- What problem are customers or the team experiencing today? (Long waits? Repeated simple questions?)
- How many contacts per day, and what are the top five reasons people contact support?
- How is success measured today (response time, resolution rate, satisfaction score)?
- Who will be affected: customers, agents, the IT team, compliance?
- What have you already tried?

If you find that 50% of contacts are "Where is my order?", a simple order-tracking page or automatic shipping emails may solve the problem better than a chatbot.

## Common mistakes

1. **Taking the request at face value.** "We need X" is a *solution*. Always dig for the underlying need.
2. **Talking only to managers.** The people doing the work every day often know exactly where the problem is.
3. **Assuming the solution must be software.** Process changes, training or policy changes are often faster and cheaper.
4. **Using technical jargon with business users** (or business jargon with developers). A BA adapts language to the audience.
5. **No definition of success.** Without a measurable goal, nobody can tell whether the change worked.
6. **Skipping the as-is.** If you don't understand how things work today, you can't design something better.
