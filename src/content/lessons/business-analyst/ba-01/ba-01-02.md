> **Prerequisites:** *What is business analysis?* You should know that a BA defines needs and recommends solutions that deliver value.

## Explanation

### One role, many job titles

"Business analyst" is a role more than a job title. You will find the same work under names such as **Business Analyst**, **Systems Analyst**, **Functional Analyst**, **Requirements Analyst**, **Process Analyst**, **Product Analyst** and, in Agile teams, sometimes **Product Owner**. When you read a job advert, ignore the title and read what the person actually does.

### The core responsibilities

Whatever the title, a BA is usually responsible for five things:

| Responsibility | What it looks like in practice |
|---|---|
| **Understand the problem** | Interviews, workshops, observing people do their work, reading existing documents |
| **Define requirements** | Writing clear statements of what the solution must do, and how well, that everyone agrees on |
| **Model and communicate** | Process maps, use cases, wireframes and user stories that make the idea visible |
| **Bridge business and technology** | Explaining technical limits to business people, and business rules to developers |
| **Support delivery and check value** | Answering developers' questions, helping test, confirming the result solves the problem |

### Where BAs work

- **Industry:** banking and insurance, healthcare, retail, telecoms, government, logistics, software companies.
- **Place in the organisation:** inside a business department (for example finance), in an IT or digital team, in a Project Management Office, or as an external consultant.
- **Type of work:** improving a business process, building or changing software, introducing a new product, or meeting a new regulation.

### How the role changes with the delivery method

| | **Traditional (Waterfall)** | **Agile (Scrum)** |
|---|---|---|
| Main output | Detailed documents such as a BRD and FRD, written early | User stories and a prioritised backlog, refined continuously |
| BA is often... | An analyst who documents before development starts | A member of the team who refines stories every sprint, sometimes acting as the Product Owner |
| Change | Controlled through a formal process | Expected and welcomed between sprints |
| Interaction | Workshops, reviews and sign-off | Daily contact with the team and frequent demos |

You will learn both ways of working later in this course. The thinking (find the real need, define it clearly, check it works) is the same in both.

### A day in the life

A typical day mixes several activities. For example:

1. **09:30**: Team stand-up. A developer asks whether a refund over £500 needs manager approval. You do not know, so you note it.
2. **10:00**: Call the operations manager to confirm the refund rule, then update the story.
3. **11:30**: Workshop with customer service staff to map how a complaint is handled today.
4. **14:00**: Draw the process map and mark the three places where work is repeated.
5. **15:30**: Review a screen mock-up with a designer against the requirements.
6. **16:30**: Update the requirements log and send a summary of decisions by email.

Notice how much of the day is **talking, listening and writing**. Technical skill matters, but communication is the core of the job.

### The skills that matter

| Group | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Analytical** | Breaking a problem down, spotting gaps and contradictions, thinking in cause and effect |
| **Communication** | Asking good questions, listening, writing clearly, presenting, facilitating a workshop |
| **Relationship** | Dealing with different personalities and competing priorities, negotiating, earning trust |
| **Technical (basic)** | Process and data modelling, SQL or Excel for checking data, understanding how systems fit together |
| **Business** | Understanding how the organisation makes money, its customers and its rules |
| **Tools** | Jira, Confluence, Visio or draw.io, Excel, PowerPoint, wireframing tools |

### Career path

A common route is: **Junior BA → Business Analyst → Senior BA → Lead BA or Principal BA**, and from there into **Product Owner, Product Manager, Project Manager, Solution Architect, Business Architect** or **Consulting**. Many BAs come from other careers such as customer service, operations, testing or finance, because **knowing the business is a strength**.

## Real-world examples

| Situation | BA's contribution |
|---|---|
| **A bank must comply with a new regulation by a fixed date** | Reads the regulation, lists exactly what must change in each process and system, and confirms with compliance that the plan covers every clause. |
| **A retailer wants a click-and-collect service** | Maps the current order process, finds that stores have no way to see online orders, and defines requirements for a store pick list and customer notifications. |
| **An insurance firm wants to reduce claim handling time** | Observes claims handlers, finds that 30% of the time is spent re-keying data between two systems, and recommends integration before any new tool is bought. |
| **A software company is building a mobile app** | Works with the product owner to turn user feedback into user stories with acceptance criteria and clarifies each story with developers during the sprint. |

## Step-by-step demonstration

**Scenario:** A manager at a regional gym chain says: *"Members keep complaining about the booking system. Can you write the requirements for a new one?"*

**Step 1: Do not accept the solution yet.** The request is "a new system". The need is still unknown. Say: *"Before we decide on a new system, can I understand what is going wrong?"*

**Step 2: Identify stakeholders.** Members, front-desk staff, class instructors, the manager, IT, and the finance team who handle payments.

**Step 3: Ask discovery questions.** For example:
- "What exactly do members complain about?"
- "How often does it happen, and how many members are affected?"
- "What do staff do when it goes wrong?"
- "What would a good booking experience look like for you?"

**Step 4: Gather evidence.** Ask for the last three months of complaints, and sit at the front desk for an hour to watch.

**Step 5: Find the real problem.** Suppose the complaints turn out to be: *"I booked a class and then it showed full when I arrived"* (double bookings), and *"I cannot cancel from my phone"* (no mobile access). The current system does not stop two people taking the last place when they book at the same moment, and it has no mobile page.

**Step 6: Write the need as a statement.**

> *Members need to book and cancel classes from their phones without being double-booked, so that class numbers are reliable and front-desk staff do not have to resolve conflicts.*

**Step 7: Recommend options.** (a) Ask the current supplier whether a fix and a mobile page are available. (b) Buy a new system. (c) Add a short-term manual rule, such as limiting online bookings to 90% of capacity. Compare cost, time and risk of each.

**Step 8: Agree the next step.** Present the options with a recommendation and let the manager decide. Only then do you write detailed requirements.

You have just done the BA's core job: **turned a solution request into a clearly stated need, with options**.

## Practical example

A coffee chain's regional manager asks: *"We want a loyalty app."*

Work through the following:

1. List **five stakeholders** (for example customers, baristas, store managers, marketing, IT).
2. Write **five questions** you would ask before agreeing to build an app (what is the goal: more visits, higher spend, or data? What do customers use now, such as paper stamp cards?).
3. Suggest **two cheaper alternatives** that might meet the same goal (for example a card-linked scheme, or a simple email offer).
4. Write a **need statement** in one sentence.

*Sample need statement:* "Regular customers need a simple way to be rewarded for repeat visits, so that the chain can increase visit frequency without adding extra work for baristas."

## Common mistakes

1. **Becoming a note-taker.** A BA who just writes down what people say, without questioning it, produces requirements that describe the wrong thing perfectly.
2. **Jumping to solutions.** Designing screens before understanding the problem is the most common and most expensive mistake.
3. **Talking only to the manager.** The people who use the process every day often know the real problems. Include them.
4. **Over-documenting or under-documenting.** Too many pages nobody reads, or too little detail so developers guess. Match the detail to the risk and to the delivery method.
5. **Ignoring the business side.** If you cannot explain how the organisation makes money and who its customers are, your recommendations will miss the point.
6. **Thinking the job ends at hand-over.** A BA stays involved until the change is working and delivering the benefit that was promised.
