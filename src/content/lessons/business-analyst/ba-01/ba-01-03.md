> **Prerequisites:** *The role of a Business Analyst.* You should know what a BA does and that the job starts with the problem, not the solution.

## Explanation

### A repeatable path from problem to value

Business analysis is not a random set of tasks. Most BA work follows a **lifecycle**: a sequence of activities that takes an idea from "something is wrong" to "the change is working and delivering value". Different organisations name the stages differently, but the shape is almost always the same.

```
 1 Understand      2 Elicit        3 Analyse and     4 Specify and     5 Validate
   the need          needs           model             document          and agree
 (why change?)   (what do people   (what is wrong,  (requirements)    (is it right?)
                   need?)           what are the
                                    options?)
                                                          |
   8 Evaluate  <-  7 Test and  <-  6 Support design  <----+
     value           accept          and build
```

This is a **cycle**, not a straight line. New facts appear and you go back. In Agile delivery you run a smaller version of the same loop for every sprint.

### The eight stages

**1. Understand the need and the context.**
Find out why the change is wanted, what the business goal is, who is affected, and what the limits are (budget, time, rules). Output: a short statement of the problem or opportunity, a list of stakeholders and a **scope**.

**2. Elicit (gather) information.**
*Elicitation* means drawing information out of people and documents. Techniques include interviews, workshops, surveys, observation, document review and prototyping. Output: raw notes, recordings, sample documents.

**3. Analyse and model.**
Study what you found. Draw the **as-is** process, find the gaps, the pain points and the root causes, then explore options for the **to-be** (future) state. Output: process maps, a gap analysis, an options comparison.

**4. Specify and document requirements.**
Write the requirements clearly enough that they can be built and tested. Output: a requirements document, user stories or a backlog, with acceptance criteria.

**5. Validate and agree.**
Check that the requirements are correct, complete and consistent, and get the stakeholders to confirm them. Output: reviewed and approved requirements, and a record of decisions.

**6. Support design and build.**
Answer questions from designers and developers, handle changes, and keep the requirements and the solution in step. Output: clarified requirements, updated stories, a managed change log.

**7. Test and accept.**
Help plan and run user acceptance testing (UAT) so the business can confirm the solution does what they asked. Output: test scenarios linked to requirements, a signed-off solution.

**8. Evaluate the value.**
After go-live, measure whether the change delivered the expected benefit. Output: a short review comparing results with the original goal.

### Traceability keeps the chain together

A good BA can answer, for any requirement: *"Which need does this come from, and which test proves it works?"* This link from need to requirement to test is called **traceability**. A simple table is often enough:

| Business need | Requirement | Test |
|---|---|---|
| Reduce refund time | REQ-12: The system shall show refund status to customers | TC-31: Request a refund, then check the status page |

### Waterfall and Agile follow the same stages

| Stage | In Waterfall | In Agile |
|---|---|---|
| Understand, elicit, analyse | Mostly done up front, before building | A short discovery at the start, then continuous refinement |
| Specify | A full document, signed off once | User stories written just in time for each sprint |
| Build and test | After the documents are approved | Every sprint produces a tested piece of the product |
| Evaluate | At the end of the project | After each release, using feedback and data |

## Real-world examples

| Stage | Online bank adding "freeze my card" |
|---|---|
| **Understand** | Customers call the helpline to block lost cards. Calls cost money and are slow at night. |
| **Elicit** | Interview call-centre staff, review 200 call logs, survey 500 customers. |
| **Analyse** | As-is: 12 minutes per call. Options: an app button, a text message service, or do nothing. |
| **Specify** | User story: *"As a cardholder, I want to freeze my card in the app so that nobody can use it while I look for it."* |
| **Validate** | Review with the fraud team, who add a rule that frozen cards still allow existing subscriptions. |
| **Support build** | Developers ask whether freezing should also stop refunds. The BA checks with the product owner. |
| **Test and accept** | Customer panel tries the feature, and the fraud team signs off. |
| **Evaluate** | Three months later: card-blocking calls are down 40%. Goal met. |

## Step-by-step demonstration

**Scenario:** A small clinic books appointments by phone. The practice manager wants to "reduce missed appointments."

**Step 1: Understand.** Ask: *How many appointments are missed? What does a missed one cost?* Suppose 15 in every 100 are missed, each costing about £40 of unused doctor time. The goal: halve missed appointments in six months.

**Step 2: Elicit.** Interview two receptionists and three doctors. Call a sample of patients who missed an appointment (with permission). Review the booking log.

**Step 3: Analyse.** Findings: patients forget (60%), cannot change the time by phone when the line is busy (25%), or have transport problems (15%). Draw the as-is flow: booking → nothing until the day → missed.

**Step 4: Options.**

| Option | Cost | Effect on the main causes |
|---|---|---|
| A. SMS reminder 24 hours before | Low | Fixes "forgot" |
| B. SMS with a reply-to-cancel option | Low-medium | Fixes "forgot" and "cannot change" |
| C. Full patient portal | High | Fixes the first two, but takes months |

Recommend **B**: it addresses 85% of the causes at a low cost.

**Step 5: Specify.** Write requirements, for example: *"The system shall send an SMS to the patient 24 hours before the appointment."* and *"If the patient replies CANCEL, the slot shall be released and shown as available."*

**Step 6: Validate.** Review with doctors and receptionists, and add a rule: patients without a mobile number are called by the receptionist.

**Step 7: Test and accept.** Create test cases: reminder sent at the right time, cancel reply works, wrong reply gets a helpful message. The practice manager signs off.

**Step 8: Evaluate.** After six months, missed appointments are 8 in 100. The goal was 7 or fewer, so the goal was nearly met. Recommend adding a second reminder.

You have followed the whole lifecycle with one simple, low-cost solution.

## Practical example

A university library wants to "stop students queuing for textbooks at the start of term."

For each stage, write **one sentence** on what you would do:

1. Understand: *(Who queues, how long, what do they need? What is the goal?)*
2. Elicit: *(Which people would you interview or observe?)*
3. Analyse: *(What could the causes be? Name two possible options.)*
4. Specify: *(Write one requirement.)*
5. Validate: *(Who would you ask to confirm it?)*
6. Test and accept: *(How would students and staff confirm it works?)*
7. Evaluate: *(Which number would you measure after the first term?)*

Compare with a partner or write down your answers in the notes panel.

## Common mistakes

1. **Skipping the first stage.** Starting with requirements before understanding the goal produces a precise description of the wrong thing.
2. **Treating the lifecycle as one-way.** New facts will force you back. Plan for it.
3. **Eliciting from only one source.** Interviews alone miss what people do but forget to mention. Combine interviews with observation and documents.
4. **Documenting without validating.** An unreviewed requirement is a guess written down.
5. **Losing traceability.** If you cannot link a requirement to a need and a test, you cannot show it was needed or that it works.
6. **Forgetting the final stage.** If nobody checks the result against the goal, the organisation never learns whether the change was worth it.
