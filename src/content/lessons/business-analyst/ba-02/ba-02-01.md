> **Prerequisites:** Module 1 (*Business Analysis Fundamentals*). You should know that a BA turns a business need into requirements that a delivery team can build and test.

## Explanation

### Why requirements matter

Studies of failed projects repeatedly point to the same cause: **unclear or missing requirements**. Developers build exactly what they understood, and if what they understood is not what the business needed, the result is rework, delay and wasted money. A requirement is the point where a business idea becomes something that can be built, tested and checked.

### What is a requirement?

The BABOK Guide defines a requirement as:

> *"A usable representation of a need."*

In plain English, a **requirement is a statement of something the business needs, written clearly enough that people can design, build and test it.** It can describe something a solution **must do** (a capability), a **quality** it must have (such as speed or security), or a **condition** it must meet (such as a law).

A requirement is **not** a solution. Compare:

| Describes a need (requirement) | Describes a solution (design) |
|---|---|
| "Customers must be able to pay for an order." | "Add a green Pay Now button using Stripe." |
| "Staff must be notified when stock falls below the reorder level." | "Send an email through Outlook every night at 6 pm." |
| "Reports must be available to managers by 8 am." | "Run a SQL Server Agent job at 5 am." |

Both kinds of statement are useful, but they belong to different people. The BA focuses on **what** is needed and **why**; designers and developers decide **how** to build it. If you write the "how" too early you limit the team's options and may lock in a poor solution.

### Where requirements come from

Requirements do not exist in one place waiting to be collected. They come from:

- **People:** customers, users, managers, support staff, regulators.
- **Documents:** policies, contracts, regulations, process manuals, previous project documents.
- **Existing systems:** reports, screens and data show what people use.
- **Observation:** what people actually do, including workarounds.
- **Strategy:** company goals, such as "reduce costs by 10%".

### The requirement hierarchy: from need to detail

Requirements exist at several levels of detail. A typical chain looks like this:

```
Business goal      "Increase online repeat purchases by 15% this year"
      |
Business need      "Customers who buy once should be encouraged to return"
      |
Requirement        "The system shall let customers save a wishlist
                    and email them when items go on sale"
      |
Detailed spec /    "When a wishlist item's price falls by 10% or more,
 user story         an email is sent within one hour"
```

Each level should be traceable to the one above. If a detailed requirement cannot be linked to any goal, ask whether it is needed.

### Characteristics of a good requirement

A good requirement is:

| Quality | Meaning | Weak example → Better |
|---|---|---|
| **Clear** | One reasonable meaning | "Fast checkout" → "Checkout completes in under 3 seconds for 95% of orders" |
| **Testable** | You can prove it is met or not | "User-friendly" → "A new user can place an order in under 2 minutes without help" |
| **Necessary** | Linked to a real need | A feature nobody asked for |
| **Feasible** | Possible within time, cost and technology | "Zero downtime forever" |
| **Consistent** | Does not contradict another requirement | One says "no login needed", another says "show the user's orders" |
| **Complete** | Contains the information needed to build and test | Missing who, what or when |
| **Atomic** | States one thing only | "The system shall log in users and send receipts" → two requirements |

You will practise writing requirements in detail later in this module.

### The requirements life cycle at a glance

Requirements are not written once and forgotten. They are **elicited, analysed, specified, validated, approved, managed through change, and finally verified** during testing. Each requirement should have an **owner**, a **status** (draft, agreed, built, tested) and a **unique ID** so it can be tracked.

## Real-world examples

| Business need | Weak requirement | Improved requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Reduce call-centre volume | "Build a self-service portal." *(solution)* | "Customers shall be able to check an order's status without contacting support." |
| Prevent fraud | "The system must be secure." *(vague)* | "The system shall require a second verification step for payments over £500." |
| Improve speed | "Make the app quicker." *(not testable)* | "The home screen shall load in under 2 seconds on a 4G connection." |
| Meet a law | "Follow GDPR." *(too broad)* | "A customer shall be able to request deletion of their personal data, and the request shall be completed within 30 days." |

## Step-by-step demonstration

**Scenario:** A pizza restaurant wants customers to order online. The owner says: *"I want a good online ordering system that works well."*

**Step 1: Ask what "good" means.** After talking to the owner and staff you learn that the goals are: fewer phone orders during the evening rush, fewer mistakes in orders, and no lost sales when the phone is engaged.

**Step 2: Identify the needs.** Customers need to place an order without calling. Kitchen staff need to receive the order clearly. The owner needs to see the day's sales.

**Step 3: Draft requirements (first attempt).**

1. The system shall be easy to use.
2. The system shall be fast.
3. The system shall take orders and send them to the kitchen and show sales.

**Step 4: Review against the qualities.** Requirement 1 is not testable. Requirement 2 is vague. Requirement 3 is three requirements in one (not atomic).

**Step 5: Rewrite.**

| ID | Requirement |
|---|---|
| R1 | A customer shall be able to select pizzas, sizes and toppings and place an order without creating an account. |
| R2 | A first-time customer shall be able to complete an order in under 3 minutes (measured in usability testing with five users). |
| R3 | The order page shall load in under 2 seconds on a typical mobile connection. |
| R4 | Each confirmed order shall appear on the kitchen screen within 10 seconds. |
| R5 | The owner shall be able to view total sales by day. |

**Step 6: Link to the need and test.** R4 comes from the need "kitchen receives orders clearly", and its test is "place an order and measure when it appears on the kitchen screen".

**Step 7: Confirm with stakeholders.** Show the table to the owner and a kitchen worker and ask: *"Is anything missing, wrong or unnecessary?"* They add: *"Orders for later delivery times."* That becomes R6, and you have found a gap before any code was written.

## Practical example

A library wants "a better way for members to renew books." Look at these draft requirements and say what is wrong with each, then rewrite it:

1. "The renewal system shall be intuitive."
2. "The system shall use a modern mobile app to renew books."
3. "The system shall allow renewals, send reminders and charge fines."
4. "The renewal shall be quick."

*Hints:* 1 is not testable. 2 describes a solution. 3 contains three requirements. 4 is vague and unmeasurable.

*A possible rewrite of 4:* "A member shall be able to renew an eligible book in no more than three taps or clicks after logging in."

## Common mistakes

1. **Writing solutions as requirements.** "Use a dropdown list" is a design choice. The requirement is "the user shall select one country from the available list."
2. **Using vague words.** "Fast", "easy", "flexible", "user-friendly" and "robust" cannot be tested. Replace them with measurable criteria.
3. **Combining several needs in one sentence.** Words like "and" and "also" often signal that a requirement should be split.
4. **Forgetting non-obvious stakeholders.** Support teams, auditors and legal staff often have requirements that nobody asked them about.
5. **Treating requirements as fixed forever.** Needs change. Keep requirements under change control and re-validate them.
6. **Not recording the source or reason.** Without it you cannot judge whether a requirement is still needed when the project is squeezed.
