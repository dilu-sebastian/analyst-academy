> **Prerequisites:** *Business, stakeholder, solution and transition requirements.* You should know that solution requirements split into functional and non-functional.

## Explanation

### Two questions about every solution

When you describe what a solution should be, you answer two different questions:

1. **What must it do?** These are **functional requirements** (FRs).
2. **How well must it do it, and under what conditions?** These are **non-functional requirements** (NFRs), sometimes called **quality attributes** or "the -ilities".

An online shop that lets customers place orders meets the functional need. If it takes 40 seconds to load, loses data, or can be hacked in an afternoon, it still fails the business. Both kinds of requirement decide whether a solution is successful.

| | **Functional** | **Non-functional** |
|---|---|---|
| Describes | Behaviour: what the system does | Quality: how well, how safely, how fast |
| Question | "What can the user do?" | "How good must it be?" |
| Example | "A customer can add an item to a basket." | "Adding an item shall take less than 1 second." |
| Test | Does the feature work? | Does it meet the measured target? |
| Often forgotten? | Rarely | Very often |

A useful rule: **functional requirements are usually verbs** (register, search, approve, send, calculate), **non-functional requirements are usually measures** (seconds, percent, users, hours, years).

### Functional requirements

Functional requirements describe the **features, data and rules** of the solution. Typical categories:

| Category | Example |
|---|---|
| **User actions** | "A user shall be able to upload a PDF document up to 10 MB." |
| **Business rules** | "A refund over £500 shall require manager approval." |
| **Calculations** | "The system shall calculate VAT at the rate valid on the order date." |
| **Data handling** | "The system shall store the customer's delivery addresses (up to five)." |
| **Reports and outputs** | "The system shall produce a monthly sales report by region." |
| **Integration** | "The system shall send each paid order to the warehouse system." |
| **Notifications** | "The system shall email the customer when the order is dispatched." |

A common format is: **"The [system/user] shall/can [do something] [under a condition]."** For example: *"When a customer's payment fails, the system shall show the reason and let the customer try another card."*

### Non-functional requirements

Non-functional requirements describe qualities. The main categories, with typical measurable examples:

| Category | What it covers | Example requirement |
|---|---|---|
| **Performance** | Speed and capacity | "Search results shall appear within 2 seconds for 95% of searches." |
| **Scalability** | Growth in users or data | "The system shall support 10,000 concurrent users and grow to 50,000 without redesign." |
| **Availability** | When it is up | "The system shall be available 99.9% of the time, measured monthly, excluding planned maintenance." |
| **Reliability** | Fewer failures | "No more than 1 failed transaction in 10,000." |
| **Security** | Protection of data and access | "Passwords shall be stored using salted hashing. Accounts lock for 15 minutes after five failed logins." |
| **Usability** | Ease of use | "A new user shall complete a booking in under 3 minutes without help." |
| **Accessibility** | Use by people with disabilities | "The site shall meet WCAG 2.1 level AA." |
| **Compatibility** | Devices, browsers, systems | "The app shall work on the latest two versions of Chrome, Safari, Edge and Firefox." |
| **Maintainability** | Ease of changing | "A new product category shall be addable by an administrator without developer help." |
| **Compliance** | Laws and standards | "Personal data shall be processed in line with GDPR." |
| **Data retention and backup** | Keeping and recovering data | "Backups taken daily and recoverable within 4 hours." |

Always include a **measure** and, where possible, **how and when it is measured**. "The system shall be secure" is not a requirement; "Accounts lock for 15 minutes after five failed login attempts" is.

### Constraints are a related idea

A **constraint** is a limit the solution must respect and that you cannot negotiate away, such as "must use the existing payroll system", "must be live by 1 April" or "budget of £200,000". Constraints often sit beside NFRs in documents, and a BA records them so options are judged against them.

### Why NFRs are the dangerous ones

Stakeholders talk naturally about features, and rarely mention quality until it fails: *"Why is it so slow?"* To draw out NFRs, ask questions about each quality:

- **Performance:** "How many people will use this at the busiest time? How long is too long to wait?"
- **Availability:** "What happens to the business if it is down for an hour?"
- **Security:** "Who must not be able to see this data? Are there laws that apply?"
- **Usability:** "Who are the users and how much training will they get?"
- **Scalability:** "What do you expect in three years?"

NFRs also **cost money**. Asking for 99.999% availability may cost many times more than 99.9%. Always show the business the cost of the target and let them decide what is worth it.

### Acceptance criteria for both types

For each requirement, write how you will know it is met:

| Requirement | Acceptance criterion |
|---|---|
| FR: "A customer can reset their password." | Given a registered email, when the customer requests a reset, then a link is emailed within 1 minute and works once. |
| NFR: "The search page shall load in under 2 seconds." | In a test with 500 simulated users, 95% of searches complete in under 2 seconds. |

## Real-world examples

| System | Functional | Non-functional |
|---|---|---|
| **Online banking** | "A customer can transfer money between own accounts." | "Transfers shall be processed within 5 seconds. Every transfer shall require two-factor authentication above £250." |
| **Hospital records** | "A doctor can view a patient's allergies and medications." | "Only treating staff shall have access. All access shall be logged. The system shall be available 24/7 with at most 10 minutes of unplanned downtime a month." |
| **Streaming service** | "A user can pause and resume a video on another device." | "Video shall start within 3 seconds on a 10 Mbps connection. The service shall handle 2 million concurrent viewers." |
| **Ticket booking** | "A customer can select seats on a seating plan." | "When tickets for a popular event go on sale, the system shall remain responsive for 100,000 users within the first minute." |

## Step-by-step demonstration

**Scenario:** A pharmacy wants an online prescription refill service.

**Step 1: List the user actions (functional).** Talk to pharmacists and patients:

| ID | Functional requirement |
|---|---|
| FR1 | A registered patient shall be able to request a refill for a prescription on their record. |
| FR2 | The pharmacist shall be able to approve or reject a refill request. |
| FR3 | The patient shall receive a notification when the refill is ready. |
| FR4 | The patient shall be able to choose a pickup pharmacy. |

**Step 2: Ask quality questions.** For each quality category, ask the pharmacist:

- *Performance:* "At peak, how many requests per hour?" → "About 300 between 8 and 9 am."
- *Availability:* "If it is down?" → "Patients cannot order, but the shop still works. Up to 2 hours a month is acceptable."
- *Security:* "What data is held?" → "Medical information, which is legally protected."
- *Usability:* "Who are the users?" → "Many elderly patients."

**Step 3: Write measurable NFRs.**

| ID | Non-functional requirement |
|---|---|
| NFR1 | The system shall handle 300 refill requests per hour with each page loading in under 3 seconds. |
| NFR2 | The system shall be available 99.7% of the time each month (about 2 hours of downtime). |
| NFR3 | Patient data shall be encrypted in storage and in transit, and accessible only to the patient and authorised pharmacy staff. |
| NFR4 | The refill request form shall meet WCAG 2.1 AA, with a minimum 16-point font option. |

**Step 4: Add acceptance criteria.** For NFR1: *"A load test with 300 simulated requests per hour shows 95% of pages loading in under 3 seconds."*

**Step 5: Check conflicts.** NFR3 (strong security) may conflict with usability for elderly users if login is complex. Raise this with stakeholders and agree a compromise, such as a simple password reset by phone with identity checks.

**Step 6: Review with the team.** The developer points out that the 99.7% target requires a standby server, which adds cost. The sponsor agrees because patient trust matters.

## Practical example

For a **food-delivery app**, write:

1. Five **functional** requirements (for example restaurant search, ordering, payment, order tracking, ratings).
2. Five **non-functional** requirements, one each for performance, availability, security, usability and compatibility. Each must contain a number.
3. One **constraint**.

Then check each non-functional requirement with the question: *"How would a tester prove this is met?"*

*An example NFR:* "The app shall show the live location of the driver, updated at least every 10 seconds, for 95% of the delivery time."

## Common mistakes

1. **Ignoring non-functional requirements** until the solution is slow, insecure or unusable.
2. **Writing NFRs without numbers.** "Fast", "secure" and "reliable" cannot be tested.
3. **Treating every quality as maximum.** 100% availability is impossible and very costly. Agree what is enough.
4. **Mixing functional and non-functional in one sentence.** "The system shall allow search and be fast" should become two requirements.
5. **Forgetting conflicts.** Security, usability and performance often pull against each other. Surface them early and let the business decide.
6. **Hiding constraints.** If the solution must integrate with an old system or meet a deadline, record it, because it limits the options.
