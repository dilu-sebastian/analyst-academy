> **Prerequisites:** *Writing good requirements.* You should be able to write clear, atomic requirements and know that stakeholders will usually ask for more than can be delivered.

## Explanation

### You cannot build everything

Every project has limited time, money and people. Stakeholders, meanwhile, ask for everything: *"All of these are important."* If every requirement is top priority, none is, and the team has no guidance when time runs short. **Prioritisation is how a BA helps the business choose what to deliver first and what to leave out.**

Prioritisation is a business decision made by stakeholders and the sponsor, not by the BA alone. The BA's job is to **provide a method, prepare the evidence, facilitate the discussion and record the decision**.

### The MoSCoW method

**MoSCoW** is one of the most widely used prioritisation techniques, especially in Agile projects (it comes from the DSDM method). It sorts requirements into four groups. The capital letters spell the name; the lowercase "o"s are just there to make it pronounceable.

| Category | Meaning | Test question |
|---|---|---|
| **M**ust have | Essential. Without it the solution fails, is illegal, unsafe or has no value. | "If this is missing, can we still go live?" If **no** → Must. |
| **S**hould have | Important, but there is a workaround or it can wait for a short time. | "Painful but not fatal?" → Should. |
| **C**ould have | Nice to have. Little impact if left out. Included only if time and budget allow. | "Would be good, but few would miss it?" → Could. |
| **W**on't have (this time) | Agreed as out of scope for **now**. It may be done later. | "Explicitly not in this release." → Won't. |

"Won't" is the most useful category for managing expectations. It does not mean "never". It means "not this time", and it protects the team from scope creep.

### A good rule of thumb for the split

DSDM suggests that **Must haves should not exceed about 60% of the planned effort**, leaving **20% for Should haves and 20% for Could haves** as a buffer. If everything in a plan is Must, the project has no room to absorb a problem.

```
 Effort available:  |=========== 60% Must ===========|== 20% Should ==|= 20% Could =|
                    ^ guaranteed delivery              ^ buffer to protect the Musts
```

If delays happen, the team drops Could and Should items first and the Musts are still delivered.

### How to decide: criteria for Must

A requirement is a Must if one or more of these is true:

- **Legal or regulatory:** the organisation breaks the law without it.
- **Safety:** people could be harmed.
- **Core value:** the solution does not achieve its main purpose.
- **Dependency:** other Must requirements cannot work without it.
- **No acceptable workaround:** the business cannot operate even temporarily without it.

Be tough. A good question to ask a stakeholder is: **"If we launch without this, will we cancel the launch?"**

### Other techniques (when MoSCoW is not enough)

| Technique | How it works | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| **Value vs effort matrix** | Plot each item by business value (high/low) and effort (high/low). Do high-value, low-effort first. | Quick wins, backlog grooming |
| **Weighted scoring** | Score each requirement against criteria such as value, risk and cost, then rank by total. | Many requirements, many stakeholders, formal decisions |
| **Kano model** | Classify as basic expectation, performance, or delighter. | Product features and customer satisfaction |
| **100-point method** | Each stakeholder distributes 100 points across the requirements. | Forcing trade-offs; shows what a group really values |
| **Ranking** | Put requirements in strict order 1, 2, 3... | A short list where ties are not acceptable |

MoSCoW is simple and quick but coarse: all Musts look equally important. Combine it with ranking within each group when needed.

### Prioritising with people

- **Prepare:** list the requirements with their IDs, the business goal each supports, and an effort estimate from the team.
- **Invite** the people who can decide and who understand the trade-offs.
- **Use criteria**, not volume. A louder voice should not win.
- **Challenge** every Must: *"What happens if we do not have this on day one?"*
- **Record** the decision, the reason and who agreed.
- **Review** priorities regularly. Business conditions change, so priorities can too.

## Real-world examples

| Project | Must | Should | Could | Won't (this time) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Online shop launch** | Browse products, basket, pay by card, order confirmation email | Guest checkout, order tracking | Wishlist, product reviews | Loyalty points, multi-language |
| **Hospital appointment SMS** | Send reminder 24 hours before, allow CANCEL reply | Allow RESCHEDULE reply | Choice of reminder time | Reminders in other languages |
| **Staff holiday system** | Submit request, manager approval, balance calculation (policy and legal) | Calendar view of team leave | Mobile app | Integration with payroll |
| **Bank mobile app** | Secure login, view balance, transfer money | Biometric login, payment history | Spending charts | Investment trading |

## Step-by-step demonstration

**Scenario:** A small bakery is building a website for pre-orders. The team has estimated that it can deliver about 40 days of work before the festive season. The list of requirements is below with effort estimates.

| ID | Requirement | Effort (days) |
|---|---|---|
| R1 | Customers can browse the product list with prices | 4 |
| R2 | Customers can choose a collection date and time | 5 |
| R3 | Customers can pay online by card | 8 |
| R4 | Customers receive an order confirmation email | 2 |
| R5 | Baker can see the day's orders in a list | 4 |
| R6 | Allergen information displayed for every product | 3 |
| R7 | Customers can create an account to see past orders | 6 |
| R8 | Customers can save favourite items | 3 |
| R9 | Gift messages on cakes | 2 |
| R10 | Customer reviews and photos | 7 |
| R11 | Delivery with route planning | 15 |

The total is 59 days, which is more than the 40 available, so we must prioritise.

**Step 1: Identify Must haves using the criteria.**
- **R1, R2, R3, R4, R5**: without them there is no working pre-order service. R3: the owner says that taking payment on collection is not acceptable because of no-shows. So R3 is Must.
- **R6** allergen information: a **legal** requirement for food businesses. Must.

Musts: R1 (4) + R2 (5) + R3 (8) + R4 (2) + R5 (4) + R6 (3) = **26 days**. That is 65% of 40 days. A little above the guideline of 60%, but the team agrees they are confident in the estimates, and the buffer is reasonable.

**Step 2: Should haves.** R7 (accounts, 6 days) helps repeat customers but guest checkout works. R9 (gift messages, 2 days) is valuable for cakes. Should: R7, R9 = 8 days.

**Step 3: Could haves.** R8 favourites (3 days). Could.

**Step 4: Won't have this time.** R10 reviews (7) and R11 delivery (15) are outside the budget for this release. They are recorded as "Won't have now; review in January."

**Step 5: Check the totals.**

| Category | Items | Days | Share of 40 days |
|---|---|---|---|
| Must | R1–R6 | 26 | 65% |
| Should | R7, R9 | 8 | 20% |
| Could | R8 | 3 | 7.5% |
| Won't | R10, R11 | 22 | not planned |

Must + Should + Could = 37 days, within 40. If a delay occurs, drop R8 then R9 first, and the Musts are safe.

**Step 6: Confirm with the owner and record the decision.** The owner challenges R9: *"Gift messages are our top seller in December."* After discussion, it is moved to Must because of its link to revenue. Must becomes 28 days (70%). The team agrees to monitor it and to drop R7 first if needed. The change and reason are recorded.

## Practical example

A university is planning an online system for booking study rooms. You have 30 days. Prioritise these items with MoSCoW, estimating if needed, and explain each Must:

| ID | Requirement |
|---|---|
| A | Students can search for free rooms by date and time |
| B | Students can book a room |
| C | Booking requires a student login |
| D | Students receive a confirmation email |
| E | Rooms are released if the student does not check in within 15 minutes |
| F | Students can see a map of the building |
| G | Library staff can block rooms for maintenance |
| H | Integration with the exam timetable |
| I | A mobile app |
| J | Room usage reports |

*Think about:* What is the core value? What is a legal or safety need (for example accessibility information)? What has a workaround (staff can email confirmations by hand at first)?

## Common mistakes

1. **Everything is Must.** If more than about 60% of effort is Must, the plan has no flexibility. Challenge each Must.
2. **Letting the loudest or most senior person decide.** Use criteria and evidence.
3. **Treating Won't as never.** Explain that it means "not this time", and record it, to avoid it reappearing as a surprise.
4. **Prioritising once and forgetting.** Review when circumstances change, such as a new regulation or a delayed supplier.
5. **Ignoring dependencies.** A Should that a Must depends on is in fact a Must.
6. **Prioritising without effort estimates.** A Could that takes one hour might be done today, while a Should that needs three months might not fit.
7. **Not recording the reason.** Later, nobody remembers why something was dropped.
