## Explanation

### Elicitation is more than "asking people what they want"

**Elicitation** means *drawing out* information. The word is deliberate: stakeholders rarely hand you complete, clear requirements. They know their pain points, habits and workarounds, but often can't describe what they need. Your job is to draw it out using the right technique, with the right people, in the right order.

Good elicitation starts with a **plan**. Without one, you interview whoever is available, miss key groups, and run out of time.

### What an elicitation plan answers

| Question | Example answer |
|---|---|
| **Why?** What do we need to learn? | How expense claims are submitted and approved today, and where delays happen |
| **Who?** Which stakeholders? | Employees, line managers, finance clerks, the CFO, IT support |
| **How?** Which techniques? | Interviews with managers, a workshop with finance, observation of clerks, a survey of employees |
| **When?** In what order and by when? | Week 1 document review; week 2 interviews; week 3 workshop |
| **What do we bring?** Preparation | Agenda, questions, current process draft, examples of claims |
| **What comes out?** Outputs | Notes, as-is process map, list of issues and draft requirements |

### Choosing a technique

| Technique | Best for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| **Interviews** | Depth, sensitive topics, senior stakeholders | Time-consuming; one viewpoint at a time |
| **Workshops** | Agreement across groups, resolving conflicts, fast progress | Dominant voices; needs strong facilitation |
| **Observation** | How work is *really* done, tacit knowledge | People behave differently when watched |
| **Surveys / questionnaires** | Many people, quantitative data, dispersed users | Shallow answers; low response rates |
| **Document analysis** | Policies, rules, existing system behaviour | Documents may be out of date |
| **Prototyping** | UI requirements, making ideas concrete | Users may think the prototype is the real system |
| **Focus groups** | Opinions and reactions from customers | Group-think |
| **Data analysis** | Volumes, trends, error rates | Data quality issues |

Most projects combine **three or four** techniques. A common pattern:

```
Document analysis → Interviews → Observation → Workshop to agree
   (background)      (depth)      (reality)     (consensus)
```

### Prepare before every session

- Read existing material first; don't make stakeholders repeat what's written down.
- Write your **objectives** for the session: what decisions or information you need.
- Prepare questions, but be ready to follow interesting answers.
- Book the right people and send the agenda in advance.
- Decide how you'll record the session (notes, whiteboard photos, recording with permission).

## Real-world examples

| Project | Elicitation plan used |
|---|---|
| **Airline baggage claims** | Analysed 6 months of claim data, observed 2 airport desks, interviewed 5 agents, held a workshop with ops and IT |
| **University timetabling** | Surveyed 2,000 students, interviewed department admins, reviewed room booking rules |
| **Retail stock replenishment** | Observed night shifts in 3 stores, interviewed regional managers, prototyped a new ordering screen |

## Step-by-step demonstration

**Scenario:** You are the BA on a project to improve how a mid-size company handles employee expense claims. Complaints: claims take 3 weeks to reimburse.

**Step 1: Set objectives.** Understand the as-is process, find where time is lost, and collect needs from each group.

**Step 2: List stakeholders.** Employees (300), line managers (40), finance clerks (4), finance manager, CFO (sponsor), IT.

**Step 3: Match techniques to stakeholders.**

| Stakeholder | Technique | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Expense policy, past reports | Document analysis | Background before talking to anyone |
| Employees | Short survey (5 questions) | Many people; we want numbers |
| Line managers | 4 interviews | Find out why approvals wait |
| Finance clerks | Observation for half a day | See real processing steps |
| Finance manager, CFO, IT | Workshop | Agree pain points and priorities |

**Step 4: Sequence and schedule.** Week 1: documents and survey. Week 2: interviews and observation. Week 3: workshop.

**Step 5: Prepare.** Draft survey questions; write an interview guide; prepare a rough as-is process map to validate in the workshop.

**Step 6: Share the plan.** Send to the sponsor for agreement so stakeholders make time.

## Practical example

Which technique would you choose first, and why?

| Situation | Technique |
|---|---|
| You need to know how 5,000 customers feel about the delivery options | Survey |
| Warehouse staff say the system is fine, but orders are often late | Observation |
| Sales and operations disagree about order rules | Workshop |
| You must understand a 15-year-old system with no current experts | Document and system analysis |
| A director has 30 minutes and strong views | Interview |
| Users can't picture how the new booking screen should work | Prototype |

## Common mistakes

1. **No plan.** You end up talking to whoever replies first.
2. **Using one technique for everything.** Interviews alone miss how work really happens.
3. **Arriving unprepared.** Stakeholders lose confidence when asked things that are already documented.
4. **Forgetting quiet or remote stakeholders.** Night shift workers, field staff and customers matter.
5. **Not confirming outputs.** Every session should end with a written summary sent for confirmation.
