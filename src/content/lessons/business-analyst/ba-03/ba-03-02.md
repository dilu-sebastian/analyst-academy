## Explanation

### Interviews are the BA's most-used technique

An interview is a structured conversation with one stakeholder (or a small group) to understand their work, problems and needs. Done well, it builds trust and uncovers detail you won't find anywhere else.

### Types of interview

| Type | Description | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| **Structured** | Fixed questions in a fixed order | Comparing answers across many people |
| **Semi-structured** | Prepared topics and questions, freedom to follow up | Most BA interviews |
| **Unstructured** | Open conversation | Early discovery, very senior stakeholders |

### Three phases of an interview

```
 BEFORE                      DURING                          AFTER
 - Objectives               - Open: intro, purpose, time    - Write up within 24h
 - Research the person      - Explore: open → probing        - Send summary for confirmation
 - Interview guide            → closed questions             - Log requirements, issues,
 - Book 45-60 min           - Close: summarise, next steps     follow-ups
```

### Question types

| Type | Purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Open** | Get the stakeholder talking | *"Can you walk me through how you process a refund?"* |
| **Probing** | Dig deeper | *"What happens if the receipt is missing?"* |
| **Closed** | Confirm facts | *"Do you process refunds every day?"* |
| **Quantifying** | Get numbers | *"Roughly how many refunds a week?"* |
| **Hypothetical** | Explore future needs | *"If you could change one thing, what would it be?"* |
| **Summarising** | Check understanding | *"So refunds over £100 need a manager, and that's where the delay is?"* |

**Funnel technique:** start broad with open questions, narrow with probing questions, and finish with closed questions to confirm.

### Questions to avoid

- **Leading**: *"You'd like an automated system, wouldn't you?"* (suggests the answer)
- **Double-barrelled**: *"How do you create and approve orders?"* (two questions at once)
- **Jargon**: *"What's your current API integration pattern?"* to a sales manager
- **Solution-first**: *"What features should the app have?"* before understanding the problem

### Useful all-purpose questions

- *"Walk me through a typical day / the last time you did X."*
- *"What's the most frustrating part of this process?"*
- *"What workarounds do you use?"*
- *"What happens when things go wrong?"*
- *"Who else should I talk to?"*
- *"Is there anything I should have asked but didn't?"*

## Real-world examples

| Interviewee | Surprising discovery |
|---|---|
| Call centre agent | Agents kept a personal spreadsheet of "tricky customers" because the CRM had no notes field |
| Hospital porter | Porters wasted an hour a shift because bed-ready alerts went only to nurses |
| Finance director | The real driver for a new system was an upcoming audit, not efficiency |
| Warehouse picker | Bin locations on screen didn't match the physical shelf labels after a redesign |

Each came from an open question such as *"What workarounds do you use?"*

## Step-by-step demonstration

**Scenario:** Interviewing Priya, a line manager, about the expense approval process.

**Before.** Objective: understand why approvals take an average of 9 days. You read the expense policy and pull approval timestamps. Guide topics: current process, volume, delays, ideas.

**Opening (3 min).**
*"Thanks for your time, Priya. I'm looking at how expense claims are approved so we can speed up reimbursement. This will take about 45 minutes. I'll take notes and send you a summary to check. Is that OK?"*

**Exploring (35 min).**
- Open: *"Can you walk me through what happens when a claim lands with you?"*
  > "I get an email, log into the finance system, check receipts, and approve."
- Probing: *"What makes some claims take longer?"*
  > "Missing receipts, mostly. And I travel a lot; I can't approve on my phone."
- Quantifying: *"How many claims a month, and how many have missing receipts?"*
  > "About 30. Maybe a third are missing something."
- Hypothetical: *"If you could change one thing?"*
  > "Approve from my phone, and have the system reject claims with no receipt before they reach me."

**Closing (5 min).**
*"Let me summarise: around 30 claims a month, a third missing receipts, and you can't approve while travelling. Did I get that right? Who else should I speak to?"*
> "Yes. Speak to Tom in finance; he chases the receipts."

**After.** Write up within 24 hours, send to Priya, and log two issues (no mobile approval, no receipt validation) and one new stakeholder (Tom).

## Practical example

Improve these poor questions:

| Poor question | Problem | Better question |
|---|---|---|
| "The current system is slow, isn't it?" | Leading | "How do you feel about the speed of the current system?" |
| "How do you raise and track orders?" | Double-barrelled | "How do you raise an order?" then "How do you track it afterwards?" |
| "What features do you want?" | Solution-first | "What are the hardest parts of your work today?" |
| "Do you have issues with month-end?" | Closed too early | "Tell me about the last month-end close. What happened?" |

## Common mistakes

1. **Talking more than listening.** Aim for the stakeholder speaking 80% of the time.
2. **Reading questions robotically.** The guide is a map, not a script; follow interesting answers.
3. **Not asking for examples.** "It's slow" means little; "last Tuesday it took 40 minutes" is evidence.
4. **No summary afterwards.** Memory fades; unconfirmed notes cause disputes later.
5. **Interviewing only managers.** Front-line staff know the real process.
6. **Promising solutions in the interview.** Capture the need; don't commit to features you can't guarantee.
