## Explanation

Interviews and workshops tell you what people **say**. Three more techniques fill the gaps: **observation** shows what people **do**, **surveys** tell you what **many** people think, and **document analysis** tells you what the organisation has **written down**.

### Observation (job shadowing)

You watch people doing their real work in their real environment.

| Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Passive** | Watch silently, take notes, ask questions afterwards | Sitting behind a call centre agent for an hour |
| **Active** | Ask questions as the work happens | *"Why did you copy that into Excel?"* |
| **Participatory** | You do the task yourself | Packing orders for a shift in the warehouse |

**Tips:**
- Explain your purpose so people don't feel inspected: *"I'm studying the process, not you."*
- Record timings, interruptions, workarounds, tools, and sticky notes on monitors.
- Watch for the **Hawthorne effect**: people work differently when observed. Observe for longer, or several times.

### Surveys and questionnaires

Used to reach many people quickly and collect **quantitative** data.

| Good survey practice | Example |
|---|---|
| Keep it short (5-10 questions, under 5 minutes) | |
| Mix closed and open questions | Rating scales plus *"What one thing would you change?"* |
| Use clear, neutral wording | Not *"How much do you love our app?"* |
| Use balanced scales | Very dissatisfied ... Very satisfied (5 points) |
| One idea per question | Not *"Is the app fast and easy?"* |
| Pilot with 2-3 people first | Catch confusing questions |
| Say how results will be used | Raises response rates |

**Question formats:** multiple choice, Likert scale (agree-disagree), ranking, numeric, open text.

### Document analysis

Reviewing existing material to understand rules, processes and systems.

| Document | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Policies and procedures | Official rules and approval limits |
| Process manuals, training guides | Intended steps |
| Existing system specs, screens, reports | Current data fields and functions |
| Contracts and SLAs | Commitments to customers and suppliers |
| Regulations | Legal requirements |
| Complaint logs, help desk tickets | Real problems and their frequency |
| Forms and templates | Data captured today |

**Tip:** documents describe the **intended** process; observation shows the **actual** process. Comparing them is powerful.

### Combining techniques: triangulation

Use more than one technique for the same question. When the documents, the interviews and the observation agree, you can be confident. When they disagree, you've found something important.

```
       Documents
        (rules)
         /   \
        /  ✓  \
 Interviews — Observation
   (say)        (do)
```

## Real-world examples

| Technique | Discovery |
|---|---|
| **Observation** in a pharmacy | Pharmacists re-typed prescriptions from one system into another for every order: 2 hours lost daily |
| **Survey** of 4,000 bank customers | 62% used the mobile app mainly to check balances; few used transfers, which changed the roadmap |
| **Document analysis** of a refund policy | Policy allowed 14-day refunds, but the system enforced 7 days, causing complaints |
| **Help desk ticket analysis** | 35% of IT tickets were password resets: led to self-service reset |

## Step-by-step demonstration

**Scenario:** A delivery company wants to know why drivers miss delivery time windows.

**Step 1: Document analysis.** Read the driver procedure manual and the SLA (deliveries within a 2-hour window). Pull 3 months of delivery data: 18% miss the window.

**Step 2: Survey.** Send a 6-question survey to 120 drivers:
1. How many deliveries do you make per shift? (number)
2. How often does your route order make sense? (Never ... Always)
3. How often do you wait at the depot before leaving? (Never ... Always)
4. What causes most late deliveries? (choose up to 2)
5. How useful is the delivery app? (1-5)
6. What one change would help most? (open text)

80 responses: 70% say route order often doesn't make sense; 55% wait at the depot over 30 minutes.

**Step 3: Observation.** Ride along with 3 drivers. You see that vans are loaded in the order parcels arrive, not in delivery order, so drivers re-sort parcels at each stop.

**Step 4: Triangulate.** Data (late deliveries), survey (route and depot complaints) and observation (loading order) all point to the depot loading process.

**Step 5: Draft requirements.**
- *Parcels shall be loaded in reverse delivery sequence.*
- *The routing system shall produce a loading list for each van at least 1 hour before departure.*

## Practical example

Fix these survey questions:

| Poor | Problem | Better |
|---|---|---|
| "Don't you agree the portal is confusing?" | Leading | "How easy or difficult is the portal to use?" (Very easy ... Very difficult) |
| "How satisfied are you with price and delivery?" | Double-barrelled | Two separate questions |
| "Rate the UX of the SPA" | Jargon | "How easy is the website to use?" |
| Scale: Good / Very good / Excellent | Unbalanced | Very poor / Poor / Neutral / Good / Very good |

## Common mistakes

1. **Observing once.** One session may be unusual; observe several times or several people.
2. **Long surveys.** Response rates and answer quality drop sharply after 5 minutes.
3. **Trusting documents blindly.** They are often out of date; verify with people and observation.
4. **Ignoring open-text answers.** They often contain the best insights.
5. **Not sharing results.** People who took part want to know what happened; share a summary.
