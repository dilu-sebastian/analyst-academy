## Explanation

### Memory fades, people leave, arguments start

Imagine a project where all requirements live in people's heads and email threads. Six months later, a developer asks: *"Why does the discount only apply to orders over £50?"* Nobody remembers. The person who decided has left. The tester assumed £40. The customer complains.

**Requirements documents** exist to give everyone one **shared, agreed, durable** source of truth.

### Five jobs a requirements document does

| Job | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Shared understanding** | Business, developers and testers read the same words |
| **Agreement** | A baseline stakeholders sign off, reducing later disputes |
| **Input for delivery** | Designers design, developers build and testers test against it |
| **Traceability** | Every feature links back to a need and forward to a test |
| **Memory** | Explains decisions and rules long after the project team has moved on, including for audits |

### The classic trio

Different documents serve different readers and levels of detail.

```
        BRD                    FRD                     SRS
  Business Requirements  Functional Requirements  Software Requirements
       Document               Document              Specification
  ─────────────────     ─────────────────────    ───────────────────
  WHY + WHAT (business) WHAT the system does     WHAT + HOW WELL, complete
  For: sponsor, business For: business + IT       For: developers, testers,
  Written by: BA         Written by: BA (+ SA)    architects, vendors
                                                  Written by: BA / systems analyst
```

| | BRD | FRD | SRS |
|---|---|---|---|
| Level | High-level business needs | Detailed system functions | Full functional + non-functional spec |
| Language | Business | Business-technical mix | Precise, technical |
| Typical content | Objectives, scope, stakeholders, business rules, high-level requirements | Use cases, functional requirements, screens, data, workflows | FRs, NFRs, interfaces, constraints, data models |
| Key question | Why are we doing this and what does the business need? | What must the system do? | Exactly what must the software do and how well? |

In practice, the names overlap and many organisations merge them (for example, a BRD plus a combined FRS/SRS). The **content** matters more than the title.

### Documents in Agile

Agile values *"working software over comprehensive documentation"*, not *"no documentation"*. In Agile teams:
- The **product backlog** of user stories replaces much of the FRD.
- A lightweight **product vision** or **lean BRD** captures goals and scope.
- **Confluence pages** hold business rules, process maps, NFRs and decisions.
- Documentation is **just enough, just in time**.

## Real-world examples

| Context | Typical documentation |
|---|---|
| **Bank core system replacement** (regulated, vendor-built) | Full BRD, FRD and SRS; formal sign-off; traceability matrix for auditors |
| **Start-up mobile app** | One-page product vision, backlog of user stories, Figma designs |
| **Government digital service** | Discovery report, user needs, backlog, NFR page, accessibility statement |
| **Outsourced development** | Detailed SRS, because it forms part of the contract with the vendor |

## Step-by-step demonstration

**Scenario:** You're the BA on a new online appointment system for a clinic chain. How much documentation do you need?

**Step 1: Identify your readers.** The sponsor (needs scope and benefits), clinic managers (need to agree rules), an external vendor (needs to build and quote), and testers.

**Step 2: Consider the context.** External vendor, fixed-price contract, patient data (regulated). These push towards **more formal** documentation.

**Step 3: Choose the set.**
- **BRD**: objectives (reduce phone bookings by 40%), scope, stakeholders, business rules (cancellation within 24 hours), high-level requirements.
- **SRS** for the vendor: detailed functional requirements, NFRs (availability, data protection), interfaces with the patient record system.
- **Traceability matrix**: links BRD requirements to SRS requirements to test cases.

**Step 4: Agree templates and sign-off.** Use the organisation's templates; agree that the operations director signs the BRD and the IT director signs the SRS.

**Step 5: Keep them alive.** Version control on every change, with a change log at the front.

## Practical example

Which document would you look in?

| Question | Document |
|---|---|
| What business problem justifies this project? | BRD |
| What happens on screen when a patient cancels? | FRD |
| What is the maximum response time for the booking API? | SRS |
| Which departments are in scope? | BRD |
| What fields does the registration form capture and validate? | FRD / SRS |

## Common mistakes

1. **Documenting for the sake of it.** Write for a reader and a purpose, not to fill a template.
2. **One document for every audience.** Executives won't read 60 pages; developers need more than bullet points.
3. **Letting documents rot.** An outdated document is worse than none because people trust it.
4. **No version control.** "Final_v3_REAL_final.docx" is a warning sign.
5. **Thinking Agile means no documentation.** Agile means appropriate, living documentation.
