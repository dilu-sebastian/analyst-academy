## Explanation

### Conflict is normal

Stakeholders have different goals, pressures and perspectives, so **conflict is inevitable** in business analysis. Sales wants flexibility, finance wants control, compliance wants caution, users want simplicity. Handled well, conflict surfaces better solutions. Handled badly, it stalls projects and damages relationships.

### Common sources of conflict

| Source | Example |
|---|---|
| **Competing priorities** | Marketing wants a loyalty feature; operations wants faster checkout first |
| **Different interpretations** | "Active customer" means different things to sales and finance |
| **Resources** | Two departments want the same developers |
| **Fear of change** | Staff worry about job security or losing expertise |
| **Personality and style** | Detail-focused vs big-picture stakeholders |
| **Unclear roles** | Two people both think they decide |
| **Past experience** | "The last system was a disaster" |

### The Thomas-Kilmann modes

Five ways people handle conflict, based on **assertiveness** (meeting your own concerns) and **cooperativeness** (meeting others' concerns):

```
 Assertive │ COMPETING                    COLLABORATING
           │ (win-lose)                   (win-win)
           │              COMPROMISING
           │              (split the difference)
           │ AVOIDING                     ACCOMMODATING
 Unassert. │ (lose-lose / postpone)       (lose-win)
           └──────────────────────────────────────────
             Uncooperative               Cooperative
```

| Mode | When it's appropriate | Risk if overused |
|---|---|---|
| **Collaborating** | Important issue, time available, need commitment | Slow |
| **Compromising** | Moderate importance, equal power, need a quick fix | Nobody fully satisfied |
| **Competing** | Urgent, safety/compliance, unpopular but necessary decision | Damages relationships |
| **Accommodating** | Issue matters more to them; build goodwill | You lose your position |
| **Avoiding** | Trivial issue; need time to cool down | Problems grow |

BAs usually aim to **facilitate collaboration**, escalating to the sponsor when needed.

### A BA's conflict resolution steps

1. **Understand each position** separately (1:1 conversations).
2. **Look for interests behind positions.** *Position:* "We need approval on every order." *Interest:* "We must prevent fraud."
3. **Find common ground**: shared goals and facts.
4. **Use data and objective criteria** (business objectives, cost, risk, evidence).
5. **Generate options** together that meet several interests.
6. **Agree** a decision or escalate with clear options.
7. **Document** the outcome and rationale.

### Positions vs interests

| Stakeholder | Position (what they say) | Interest (why) |
|---|---|---|
| Finance | "All orders over £100 need approval" | Prevent fraud and overspending |
| Sales | "No approvals; they slow us down" | Close deals fast; hit targets |
| **Option meeting both** | Auto-approve orders under £1,000 for trusted customers; approval only above that or for new customers | Fraud controlled; most deals fast |

### Difficult stakeholder types and approaches

| Type | Behaviour | Approach |
|---|---|---|
| **The blocker** | Says no to everything | Understand their concerns 1:1; involve them early; give them ownership of part of the solution |
| **The absent stakeholder** | Never available | Make it easy: short slots, prepared options; agree a delegate; escalate impact |
| **The dominator** | Takes over meetings | Ground rules; structured techniques (silent brainstorming); talk 1:1 |
| **The changer** | Keeps changing their mind | Document decisions; show impact of changes; use change control |
| **The solution-pusher** | "Just build X" | Ask about the problem; compare options objectively |
| **The silent one** | Agrees in meetings, complains later | Ask directly; 1:1 follow-ups; anonymous input |
| **The expert hoarder** | Withholds knowledge | Recognise their expertise; show how sharing helps them |

### Negotiation basics

- Separate the **people** from the **problem**.
- Focus on **interests**, not positions.
- Invent **options for mutual gain**.
- Use **objective criteria**.
- Know your **BATNA** (Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement): e.g. escalate to the sponsor.

*(From Fisher and Ury, "Getting to Yes".)*

### When to escalate

Escalate to the sponsor or decision-maker when:
- Stakeholders can't agree after a genuine attempt
- The conflict affects scope, budget, schedule or compliance
- It's outside your authority

**Escalate with options, not just problems:** *"Sales and finance disagree on approval limits. Options: A) £500 limit, B) £1,000 for trusted customers, C) keep current. Impact of each attached. Recommendation: B."*

## Real-world examples

| Situation | Resolution |
|---|---|
| Two departments defined "customer" differently | BA facilitated a workshop with data; agreed two defined terms ("account holder", "active buyer") in the glossary |
| Warehouse manager blocked a new system | BA discovered fear of losing control over stock; gave him ownership of configuring stock rules; he became a champion |
| Director kept changing priorities weekly | BA introduced a visible backlog with impact estimates; changes slowed once trade-offs were visible |

## Step-by-step demonstration

**Scenario:** On the gym project, the head of membership wants members to cancel online **up to 30 minutes** before class. The operations manager insists on **4 hours** so instructors can plan.

**Step 1: Meet separately.**
- Membership: worried members will be frustrated; flexibility reduces cancellations of membership.
- Operations: worried about empty spaces and instructor planning.

**Step 2: Identify interests.** Membership: member satisfaction and retention. Operations: full classes, efficient staffing.

**Step 3: Gather data.** 70% of cancellations happen within 4 hours; waitlists fill 85% of freed places if released at least 1 hour before class.

**Step 4: Generate options together.**
- A) 4 hours (operations' position)
- B) 30 minutes (membership's position)
- C) 2 hours, with freed places auto-offered to waitlist
- D) 1 hour, but late cancellations count towards a no-show limit

**Step 5: Evaluate against objectives.** Option C balances satisfaction and fill rate; data shows most freed places will be filled.

**Step 6: Agree.** Both accept C, with a review after 3 months of data.

**Step 7: Document** the decision in the decisions log and update acceptance criteria.

## Practical example

A stakeholder says in a workshop: *"This whole project is a waste of time. The old system works fine."* How do you respond?

1. Stay calm and acknowledge: *"Thanks for being direct; it's important we hear concerns."*
2. Explore: *"What works well for you in the current system? What would you worry about losing?"*
3. Park the debate if it derails the session; offer a 1:1 conversation.
4. In the 1:1, listen for interests (fear of extra work, past failures).
5. Involve them in protecting what works (e.g. "Help us make sure the new process keeps these strengths").

## Common mistakes

1. **Avoiding conflict.** Problems grow and surface late.
2. **Taking sides.** The BA's credibility depends on neutrality.
3. **Arguing positions.** Dig for interests.
4. **Escalating too early or without options.** Try to resolve first; then escalate with recommendations.
5. **Not documenting decisions.** The same argument returns next month.
