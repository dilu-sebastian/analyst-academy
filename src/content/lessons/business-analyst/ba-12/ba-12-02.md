## Explanation

### Knowing isn't enough; you must explain

Conceptual questions test whether you understand BA ideas well enough to **explain them clearly** and **apply them**. Interviewers aren't looking for a memorised definition; they want to hear that you've used the idea, or could.

### The DEFINE-EXAMPLE-WHY structure

A simple 3-part answer works for almost every conceptual question:

```
 1. DEFINE     a clear one- or two-sentence definition in plain English
 2. EXAMPLE    a concrete example (ideally from your experience or a project)
 3. WHY / HOW  why it matters, or how you use it in practice
```

**Example: "What is a non-functional requirement?"**
> **Define:** A non-functional requirement describes *how well* a system must perform, such as speed, security, availability or usability, rather than what it does.
> **Example:** For an online banking app, *"transfers require two-factor authentication"* and *"pages load in under 2 seconds for 95% of users"*.
> **Why:** Stakeholders rarely mention them, but missing NFRs cause failures like sites crashing on launch day, so I use a checklist of categories and make each one measurable.

Aim for **45-90 seconds** per answer.

### "Difference between" questions

Many conceptual questions ask you to compare. Use a mini-table in your head: **purpose, audience/owner, example**.

| Question | Short answer |
|---|---|
| BRD vs FRD | BRD = why and what the business needs (for business). FRD = what the system must do in detail (for business and IT). |
| Use case vs user story | Use case = detailed flow including alternates and exceptions. User story = short, value-focused prompt for a conversation, with AC. |
| Verification vs validation | Verification = requirements built right (quality). Validation = right requirements (meet the need). |
| BA vs PO | BA = analysis specialist across needs, models, stories. PO = accountable for product value and backlog order. |
| Include vs extend | Include = always happens. Extend = optional, under a condition. |
| AC vs DoD | AC = story-specific behaviour. DoD = team-wide quality standard. |
| Agile vs Waterfall | Iterative, adaptive, frequent delivery vs sequential phases with up-front requirements. |

### Other concepts interviewers like

| Concept | One-line explanation |
|---|---|
| **SWOT** | Strengths, Weaknesses (internal), Opportunities, Threats (external) |
| **PESTLE** | Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Legal, Environmental factors |
| **Feasibility study** | Assessing whether a solution is technically, financially and operationally viable |
| **Business case** | Justification for a change: options, costs, benefits, risks, recommendation |
| **Scope creep** | Uncontrolled growth in scope; managed through change control and prioritisation |
| **Change request** | A formal request to change baselined scope or requirements, assessed for impact |
| **UAT** | Business users test the solution against their needs before go-live |
| **KPI** | Measure of progress towards an objective |
| **MVP** | Smallest product that delivers value and enables learning |

### If you don't know the answer

Be honest and reason aloud:
> *"I haven't used PESTLE directly, but I understand it's a framework for analysing external factors. I'd expect it to be used in strategy work, for example checking how new regulations or economic changes affect a product. I've used SWOT for a similar purpose."*

This shows honesty, reasoning and related knowledge, which is better than bluffing.

## Real-world examples

| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| "MoSCoW is Must, Should, Could, Won't." | "MoSCoW is a prioritisation technique: Must, Should, Could and Won't have this time. I test each Must by asking if we'd cancel the launch without it, and keep Musts to about 60% of effort for contingency. On my course project, it cut the first release from 18 to 9 features." |
| "A traceability matrix tracks requirements." | "It links each requirement to its source, design and test cases. Forward tracing shows every requirement is tested; backward tracing catches work nobody asked for. I'd also use it for impact analysis when a change is requested." |

## Step-by-step demonstration

**Question:** *"What's the difference between functional and non-functional requirements?"*

**Step 1: Define both.** *"Functional requirements describe what the system does; non-functional requirements describe how well it does it."*

**Step 2: Give a paired example.** *"For a password reset: functional is 'the user can reset their password by email'; non-functional is 'the reset email arrives within 60 seconds' and 'the link expires after 30 minutes'."*

**Step 3: Explain why it matters / how you work.** *"NFRs are often forgotten because users assume them, so I use a category checklist (performance, security, availability, usability, accessibility) and make each one measurable so testers can verify it."*

**Step 4: Stop.** About 60 seconds. Let the interviewer follow up.

## Practical example

Practise Define-Example-Why for:

1. "What is a user story?"
2. "What is BPMN and why use it?"
3. "What is a stakeholder?"
4. "What is gap analysis?"
5. "What is the role of a BA in UAT?"

Model for #5:
> **Define:** UAT is when business users test the solution against their real needs before go-live. **Example:** For a booking system, receptionists ran scenarios like booking on behalf of a member and cancelling within 2 hours. **How:** As a BA I help prepare scenarios from the acceptance criteria, support testers, triage issues into defects versus change requests, and confirm with the business owner that the solution is ready.

## Common mistakes

1. **Textbook definitions only.** Always add an example and why it matters.
2. **Rambling.** Keep to 45-90 seconds, then stop.
3. **Jargon without explanation.** Explain terms as you would to a stakeholder.
4. **Bluffing.** Admit gaps and reason from related knowledge.
5. **Ignoring the company's context.** Use examples from their industry where possible.
