> **Prerequisites:** ba-12-03 Answering scenario questions

## Explanation

### Past behaviour predicts future behaviour

**Behavioural questions** ask you to describe real situations from your past: *"Tell me about a time when..."*, *"Give me an example of..."*, *"Describe a situation where..."*. Interviewers believe that how you acted before is the best guide to how you'll act in their team.

### The STAR method

STAR gives every story a clear, complete shape:

| Letter | Meaning | Content | Share of answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| **S**ituation | Context | Where, when, what was happening | ~15% |
| **T**ask | Your responsibility | What *you* needed to achieve | ~10% |
| **A**ction | What you did | Specific steps *you* took, techniques used | ~55% |
| **R**esult | Outcome | What happened, ideally with numbers; what you learned | ~20% |

**Action is the heart of the answer.** Most weak answers spend too long on the situation and too little on what they actually did.

### Make it strong

| Tip | Example |
|---|---|
| Say **"I"**, not just "we" | "I scheduled 1:1s with both managers" |
| Name **techniques** | "I used a power-interest grid", "I ran a silent brainstorm" |
| **Quantify** results | "cut processing time from 9 to 4 days" |
| Include a **lesson** | "Since then, I always validate assumptions with data early" |
| Keep it to **~2 minutes** | Practise with a timer |
| Choose **relevant** stories | Match to the competency being tested |

### STAR+L for failure questions

For *"Tell me about a mistake / failure"*, add **L for Learning** and spend more time on it:
- Pick a real but not catastrophic mistake.
- Own it (no blaming others).
- Show what you did to fix it.
- Show what you changed afterwards.

### Common behavioural questions for BAs

| Competency | Question |
|---|---|
| Stakeholder management | "Tell me about a time you dealt with a difficult stakeholder." |
| Conflict | "Describe a time two stakeholders disagreed. What did you do?" |
| Elicitation | "Give an example of how you uncovered a requirement others missed." |
| Influence | "Tell me about a time you persuaded someone without authority." |
| Ambiguity | "Describe a time you had to work with unclear requirements." |
| Pressure | "Tell me about a time you had a tight deadline." |
| Failure | "Tell me about a mistake you made and what you learned." |
| Process improvement | "Give an example of a process you improved." |
| Communication | "Describe a time you explained something complex to a non-technical audience." |
| Teamwork | "Tell me about working closely with developers or testers." |

### No BA experience yet?

Use examples from:
- Previous jobs (customer service, operations, admin, retail, teaching, hospitality)
- Volunteering or clubs (organising events, improving processes)
- Education (group projects, dissertations)
- This course's projects and assignments (a strong source: they're real BA work)

Interviewers care about **behaviour and thinking**, not the job title you had.

## Real-world examples

**Weak answer** to *"Tell me about a time you dealt with a difficult stakeholder"*:
> *"We had a manager who was difficult. We kept trying to talk to him, and eventually things got better and the project went fine."*

Problems: vague, "we", no actions, no result, no learning.

**Strong answer (STAR):**
> **S:** In my previous role as an operations coordinator at a logistics firm, we were introducing a new delivery scheduling tool. The warehouse manager was openly against it and missed every requirements meeting.
> **T:** I was asked to gather requirements from the warehouse team and needed his input on loading rules, which only he fully knew.
> **A:** I asked for a 15-minute 1:1 at the start of his shift instead of a long meeting. I listened first and learned he'd been blamed when the last system failed, and feared losing control of loading decisions. I showed him the draft process map and asked him to correct the loading steps, so he could shape that part. I also suggested he lead the pilot in his warehouse.
> **R:** He corrected three loading rules we'd misunderstood, which would have caused missed deliveries. He became the pilot lead, and the warehouse hit 96% on-time loading in the first month, up from 88%. I learned that resistance often hides a reasonable fear, and that giving people ownership works better than persuasion.

About 2 minutes, mostly Action, with numbers and a lesson.

## Step-by-step demonstration

**Question:** *"Tell me about a time you had to work with unclear requirements."*

**Step 1: Choose a story.** Course project: building requirements for a library app with a vague brief, "make the library more popular with young people".

**Step 2: Draft STAR notes.**
- **S:** Course case study, 5 branches, membership of 16-24s down 35%.
- **T:** Turn a vague goal into clear, prioritised requirements in two weeks.
- **A:** Wrote a problem statement and SMART objective; identified stakeholders with an onion diagram; surveyed 40 students; found top barriers were opening hours and an old website; ran five whys; compared three options with weighted scoring; wrote 12 user stories with AC and prioritised with MoSCoW.
- **R:** Clear MVP of 5 stories (mobile catalogue, evening study hours, event notifications); assessor feedback praised the evidence-based prioritisation. Learned to turn vague goals into measurable objectives first.

**Step 3: Practise aloud** and trim the Situation to two sentences.

**Step 4: Prepare follow-ups.** *"What would you do differently?"* → *"Interview librarians earlier; I relied too heavily on the survey."*

## Practical example

Map your story bank to competencies. One story can answer several questions if you shift the emphasis.

| Story | Can answer |
|---|---|
| Warehouse manager resistance | Difficult stakeholder; influence without authority; uncovering hidden requirements |
| Library case study | Unclear requirements; prioritisation; data-driven decisions |
| Missed a stakeholder (payroll) | Failure/mistake; stakeholder identification; recovering from problems |
| Process improvement at work | Process improvement; using evidence; results under pressure |

Aim for **8-10 stories** covering all the competencies in the table above.

## Common mistakes

1. **Too much Situation.** Get to your actions quickly.
2. **"We" instead of "I".** The interviewer is hiring you.
3. **No result or no numbers.** Even estimates help: "about 20% fewer calls".
4. **Hypothetical answers.** "I would..." answers a scenario question, not a behavioural one; give a real example.
5. **Choosing a fake weakness.** "I work too hard" isn't credible; choose a genuine, manageable development area.
6. **Not practising aloud.** Stories that look good on paper often run long when spoken.
