> **Prerequisites:** none. This is the very first lesson. You only need curiosity and a willingness to ask "why?".

## Explanation

### What is data?

**Data** is simply recorded facts about something that happened. Every time you buy a coffee, a till records *what* you bought, *when*, *how much* you paid and *how* you paid. That record is data.

On its own, one record tells you very little. But put together thousands of records and patterns start to appear: the busiest hour of the day, the most popular drink, the customers who come back every week.

### What is data analytics?

**Data analytics is the process of collecting, cleaning, exploring and interpreting data so that people can make better decisions.**

Notice the last part. The goal of analytics is never "a nice chart" or "a clever SQL query". The goal is a **decision**: open a new store or not, order more stock or less, change a price, hire another support agent.

A useful way to remember it is the **DIKW ladder**:

| Level | What it is | Coffee-shop example |
|---|---|---|
| **Data** | Raw facts, no context | `2024-03-04 08:12, Latte, £3.40, card` |
| **Information** | Data organised and summarised | "We sold 412 lattes last week." |
| **Knowledge** | Information compared and explained | "Latte sales are 30% higher on weekdays, driven by the 8-9am commuter rush." |
| **Wisdom / Decision** | Action based on knowledge | "Schedule an extra barista from 7:30 to 9:30 on weekdays." |

A data analyst's job is to **move people up this ladder**.

### Why do companies need data analysts?

Businesses collect huge amounts of data, but most managers do not have the time or the skills to turn it into answers. Without analysis, decisions are made on gut feeling, which is often wrong. An analyst:

1. Turns a vague worry ("sales feel slow") into a precise question ("Did revenue fall last month compared with the same month last year, and in which regions?").
2. Finds and prepares the right data.
3. Answers the question with evidence.
4. Explains the answer in plain language, with a clear recommendation.

## Real-world examples

| Industry | Question | Data used | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Retail** | Which products should we put on promotion next month? | Sales history, stock levels, margins | Promote high-margin items with excess stock |
| **Banking** | Which card transactions might be fraud? | Transaction amounts, locations, times | Block and verify unusual transactions |
| **Healthcare** | Why are patients missing appointments? | Booking records, reminders sent, travel distance | Send SMS reminders 24h before |
| **Streaming** | Which shows should we recommend to this user? | Viewing history, ratings | Personalised home screen |
| **Logistics** | Why are deliveries late in one city? | Delivery times, routes, driver schedules | Re-plan routes and shift start times |
| **HR** | Why are new employees leaving in their first year? | Exit surveys, tenure, team, manager | Improve onboarding in the teams with highest churn |

In every row the pattern is the same: **question, data, analysis, decision.**

## Step-by-step demonstration

Let's walk through a tiny real analysis the way a professional would. A small bakery owner says: *"I think we are wasting too much bread."*

**Step 1: Turn the worry into a question.**
"How many loaves do we throw away each day, and on which days is waste highest?"

**Step 2: Find the data.** The owner has a simple daily log:

| Day | Baked | Sold | Wasted |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | 80 | 71 | 9 |
| Tue | 80 | 74 | 6 |
| Wed | 80 | 77 | 3 |
| Thu | 80 | 75 | 5 |
| Fri | 80 | 79 | 1 |
| Sat | 80 | 80 | 0 |
| Sun | 80 | 52 | 28 |

**Step 3: Check the data.** Does `Baked - Sold = Wasted` on every row? Yes. Good, the data is consistent. (In real life you will often find rows that don't add up, and that is your first finding.)

**Step 4: Analyse.**
- Total wasted = 9 + 6 + 3 + 5 + 1 + 0 + 28 = **52 loaves per week**.
- Sunday alone = 28 loaves, which is **54% of all waste**.
- Saturday sold out completely, so customers may have been turned away.

**Step 5: Interpret.** The problem isn't "too much bread" in general. It is **too much bread on Sunday, and possibly too little on Saturday.**

**Step 6: Recommend.** Bake about 55 loaves on Sunday and test 85-90 on Saturday. Track for four weeks and compare.

**Step 7: Communicate.** One sentence for the owner: *"More than half of your weekly waste happens on Sunday; baking 25 fewer loaves on Sundays would save around 100 loaves a month, and Saturday may be under-supplied."*

That is data analytics. No advanced tools, just a clear question, checked data, simple maths and a clear recommendation.

## Practical example

Try this yourself with the data below. A gym tracks how many members visit each hour on a weekday:

| Hour | Visits |
|---|---|
| 06:00 | 45 |
| 07:00 | 80 |
| 08:00 | 60 |
| 12:00 | 55 |
| 17:00 | 95 |
| 18:00 | 120 |
| 19:00 | 70 |
| 21:00 | 15 |

Ask yourself:
1. What is the peak hour? *(18:00 with 120 visits.)*
2. What decision could the manager make? *(Add staff or open extra equipment from 17:00 to 19:00; schedule cleaning and maintenance at 21:00 when it is quiet.)*
3. What data is **missing** that you would want before deciding? *(Weekend figures, capacity of the gym, how long people stay, whether 18:00 is crowded enough to make members leave.)*

Asking "what's missing?" is one of the most valuable habits an analyst can build.

## Common mistakes

1. **Starting with the tool instead of the question.** Opening Excel and "seeing what's there" wastes hours. Always write the question down first.
2. **Confusing information with insight.** "We sold 412 lattes" is a number. "Latte sales drop 40% when it rains" is an insight because it explains something and suggests an action.
3. **Not checking the data.** If totals don't add up or dates are missing, every conclusion built on top is unreliable.
4. **Assuming correlation means cause.** Ice-cream sales and sunburn both rise in summer; ice cream does not cause sunburn. (You'll study this properly in the Statistics module.)
5. **Hiding the answer in jargon.** Managers need a clear sentence and a recommendation, not a list of every calculation you ran.
6. **Ignoring what the data can't tell you.** Always state the limits of your analysis.
