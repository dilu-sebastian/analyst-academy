> **Prerequisites:** *What is data analytics?* You should know the difference between data, information and a decision.

## Explanation

### Four questions, four types of analytics

Every analysis answers one of four questions. Each one needs more skill than the last and gives more value:

| Type | Question it answers | Looks at | Typical output |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Descriptive** | *What happened?* | The past | Reports, dashboards, totals, averages |
| **Diagnostic** | *Why did it happen?* | The past, in more detail | Breakdowns, comparisons, root-cause findings |
| **Predictive** | *What is likely to happen?* | The future, using patterns from the past | Forecasts, risk scores, probabilities |
| **Prescriptive** | *What should we do about it?* | The future, with options | Recommendations, optimised plans |

Think of a doctor. **Descriptive**: "Your temperature is 39°C." **Diagnostic**: "The blood test shows a bacterial infection." **Predictive**: "Without treatment this will probably get worse within two days." **Prescriptive**: "Take this antibiotic for seven days."

### Descriptive analytics: what happened?

This is where almost every analyst starts, and where most day-to-day work lives. You summarise data with counts, totals, averages, percentages and charts.

Examples: monthly revenue, number of support tickets last week, average delivery time, the share of customers who bought twice.

Descriptive work is **not "basic"**. Getting a number that everyone trusts takes careful definitions (what counts as a "sale"? does a returned item count?) and clean data.

### Diagnostic analytics: why did it happen?

Once a number moves, someone asks why. Diagnostic analysis **breaks the number into parts** and compares them:

- By **time**: did it drop suddenly or gradually?
- By **segment**: which region, product, channel or customer type?
- By **comparison**: this month vs last month, this year vs last year, this store vs the others.

The aim is to find where the change is concentrated. If total revenue fell 10% but one region fell 40% and the others were flat, you have found where to look.

Be careful with "why". Data usually shows *where* and *when*; the *real reason* often needs a conversation with the people who run the business (a competitor opened nearby, a website bug, a supply problem). Good analysts form a hypothesis from the data and then check it.

### Predictive analytics: what will happen?

Predictive analytics uses historical patterns to estimate the future: next month's sales, which customers are likely to cancel, which loans may default.

At entry level you will mostly meet **simple forecasting** (extending a trend, adjusting for seasonality) and the *idea* behind more advanced machine-learning models. Two rules always apply:

1. A prediction is a **range of likely outcomes**, never a promise.
2. It only works if the future resembles the past. A pandemic, a new competitor or a price change can break the pattern.

### Prescriptive analytics: what should we do?

The final step turns insight into a recommended action, often by comparing options: "If we raise the price by 5%, we expect revenue to rise 3% but customer numbers to fall 2%. Option B (a loyalty discount) keeps customers and costs less."

Many prescriptive decisions are made by people using good analysis rather than by software. Your job is to present **options, trade-offs and a clear recommendation**.

### They build on each other

You usually move down the list during one project. A manager asks "why are sales down?" and you will first describe (how much, where), then diagnose (what changed), maybe predict (will it continue?) and finally prescribe (what to do).

## Real-world examples

| Business | Descriptive | Diagnostic | Predictive | Prescriptive |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Online shop** | Revenue was £120k last month | Mobile revenue fell 25% after the checkout redesign | Revenue will be about £105k next month if the trend continues | Roll back the checkout on mobile |
| **Hospital** | 18% of appointments were missed | Missed rates are highest for morning slots and patients living far away | Patients with 2+ previous no-shows are likely to miss again | Send reminders and offer video calls to this group |
| **Bank** | 1,200 card transactions were flagged | Most flags came from one merchant category abroad | Transactions with this pattern are 40 times more likely to be fraud | Block and call the customer when the pattern appears |
| **Airline** | Flights were 87% full on average | Mid-week morning flights are 55% full | Demand for the route will rise in July | Cut mid-week morning flights and add weekend ones |

## Step-by-step demonstration

Let's use the **retail database** built into this app to practise the first two types. The `orders` table has one row per order. Edit and run each query. You can change them freely, nothing you do can break the database.

**Step 1: Descriptive: how many orders per month in 2024?**

```sqlrun
SELECT substr(order_date, 1, 7) AS month,
       COUNT(*) AS orders
FROM orders
WHERE order_date >= '2024-01-01'
GROUP BY month
ORDER BY month;
```

You should see 12 rows. This answers "what happened?". You may notice that the last two months are higher than the rest.

**Step 2: Diagnostic: is that peak the same in every sales channel?**

```sqlrun
SELECT substr(order_date, 1, 7) AS month,
       channel,
       COUNT(*) AS orders
FROM orders
WHERE order_date >= '2024-10-01'
GROUP BY month, channel
ORDER BY month, channel;
```

Now the number is **broken down by channel**. Look at which channel contributes most of the rise. That is the diagnostic move: split the total until you see where the change lives.

**Step 3: Predictive (idea only).** If November and December have been higher than the rest of the year in *both* 2023 and 2024, a sensible forecast for next November is "higher again", with a range. You would also check whether anything changed (a new competitor, a promotion).

**Step 4: Prescriptive (idea only).** If seasonal peaks always arrive in the last two months, a recommendation could be: "Hire seasonal staff from mid-October and order extra stock of the best sellers by early October."

Notice that Steps 1 and 2 needed only simple SQL. Most of the value in analytics comes from asking the next question, not from complex tools.

## Practical example

A coffee chain's weekly sales dropped from £50,000 to £44,000. Classify each of the following as descriptive, diagnostic, predictive or prescriptive:

1. "Sales fell by £6,000, or 12%, last week."
2. "The drop is entirely in the three stores near the university, which were closed for exam week."
3. "If students stay away again next month, we expect about £40,000 a week."
4. "Reduce staff hours at those three stores during exam weeks and promote a delivery offer instead."

*Answers: 1 descriptive, 2 diagnostic, 3 predictive, 4 prescriptive.*

Then try to write your own set of four statements for a business you know well, such as a gym or a food-delivery app.

## Common mistakes

1. **Jumping to prediction before describing.** If you cannot correctly describe the past, a forecast built on it will be wrong. Always start with trustworthy descriptive numbers.
2. **Stopping at descriptive.** A dashboard that shows *what* but never *why* leaves managers guessing. Always ask the next question.
3. **Presenting a prediction as a fact.** Say "we expect between £40k and £45k", not "sales will be £42k".
4. **Confusing a pattern with a cause.** Diagnostic findings show where a change sits. Confirm the reason with the people who know the business before you state it.
5. **Recommending without options.** Prescriptive advice is stronger when you show the alternatives you considered and the trade-offs.
